From Roots to Rise · 2027–2031

A People’s Covenant for Ibarapa East

A practical, people-centred legislative agenda for a more productive, connected, secure and accountable constituency.

A message to the people

Leadership must be measured by what it makes possible

My fellow people of Ibarapa East,

Our constituency is rich in people, enterprise, culture, agricultural potential and community spirit. From Eruwa to Lanlate, and across our wards and rural settlements, residents work hard, raise families, educate their children, build businesses and sustain the local economy. Yet hard work must be matched by public institutions that listen, fair opportunities, dependable infrastructure and representation that takes responsibility.

I believe public office must mean more than occupying a seat or appearing at ceremonies. It must mean listening carefully, understanding the problems people face, asking difficult questions where public decisions are made, and following issues until residents receive clear answers. Representation should be visible in the quality of engagement, the seriousness of legislative work and the honesty of public reporting.

This manifesto is my proposed covenant with Ibarapa East: a commitment to make the constituency’s needs heard, to pursue practical solutions through lawful legislative channels, and to report progress without exaggeration. It is built on a simple conviction: development should be judged not by the size of announcements, but by the difference that sound policies, responsible budgets and properly delivered public services make to everyday life.

If entrusted with the responsibility of representing Ibarapa East in the Oyo State House of Assembly, I will approach the office as a duty of service. I will work to strengthen the voice of our communities, scrutinise decisions that affect them, build constructive relationships with relevant institutions and insist that public commitments are explained and monitored.

Our future cannot be built by promises alone. It requires evidence, planning, cooperation, discipline and accountability. That is the standard this document sets out.

The purpose

What representation should deliver

A state legislator does not personally control every road, school, hospital, security operation or employment programme. The office does, however, carry important responsibilities: making laws, representing constituents, scrutinising government, reviewing public expenditure, raising motions and questions, participating in committees, receiving petitions and advocating for fair attention to the constituency.

A stronger voice

Ensure that the experiences of residents, farmers, traders, young people, educators, health workers, artisans, traditional institutions and community organisations inform legislative debate and public policy.

Better scrutiny

Examine proposed budgets, programmes and public commitments with attention to need, value for money, fairness, implementation responsibilities and measurable outcomes.

Practical access

Make constituency engagement more organised so that residents can raise concerns, submit petitions, obtain referrals and understand which public institution is responsible for a particular issue.

Public accountability

Explain what has been proposed, what has been formally approved, what is being implemented, what has been completed and what remains unresolved.

Seven-pillar programme

A development agenda built around everyday needs

The seven pillars below provide the policy framework. They are connected: agricultural growth needs roads and markets; education needs safe facilities and skilled teachers; enterprise needs electricity, skills and access to customers; and community safety depends on trust, prevention and effective public institutions.

01 · Pillar one

Legislative governance and people-centred representation

The promise of representation is that people should not have to struggle to be heard. Constituency service must be organised, accessible and fair. The needs of every ward and community deserve attention, not only those that are easiest to reach or most visible.

Proposed actions

  • Establish regular constituency listening sessions and accessible channels for residents to submit concerns, petitions and policy suggestions.
  • Maintain a structured constituency issues register, recording the concern, responsible agency, referral or legislative action taken, response received and outstanding steps.
  • Use motions, questions, petitions, committee work and public hearings, where appropriate, to bring verified constituency issues before the House.
  • Scrutinise proposed budgets and public programmes for fairness, local relevance, transparent criteria and value for money.
  • Publish periodic reports on legislative activity, constituency engagements, agency responses and unresolved matters.

How the office will be judged

By the regularity of public engagement, the quality of legislative representation, the traceability of petitions and referrals, and the clarity of public reporting—not by publicity volume.

02 · Pillar two

Agriculture, food security and the rural economy

Farmers deserve an economy that rewards their work. Agriculture is not only about production. It is also about access to inputs, extension advice, storage, transport, processing, finance and dependable markets. When farmers lose value after harvest or struggle to move produce, the whole local economy feels the effect.

Proposed actions

  • Advocate for better access to agricultural extension services, relevant information, improved inputs and appropriate farming methods.
  • Promote stronger links between farmers, cooperatives, processors, buyers and existing public or private agricultural support programmes.
  • Keep rural access roads, drainage and transport links on the constituency’s infrastructure advocacy agenda, recognising their importance to farm-to-market movement.
  • Encourage practical post-harvest measures, storage, aggregation and small-scale processing that can reduce avoidable losses and improve local value addition.
  • Support transparent information about agricultural grants, credit and intervention programmes so that eligible residents can understand application requirements.
  • Promote opportunities for young people and women across farming, processing, logistics and agricultural services.

How the office will be judged

By documented farmer consultations, issues raised with relevant agencies, programme information shared, responses received and evidence of policy or budget scrutiny. Any intervention will be reported according to its actual approval and delivery status.

03 · Pillar three

Youth opportunity, skills and the digital economy

Our young people need pathways into productive work, not empty assurances. Talent is present in our communities, but talent must meet opportunity. Skills programmes are useful when they connect to real demand, recognised training, practical experience and routes into employment or enterprise.

Proposed actions

  • Advocate for vocational, technical and digital skills programmes informed by local employer, artisan and enterprise needs.
  • Encourage apprenticeship and mentorship partnerships with credible businesses, professionals, tradespeople and training institutions.
  • Promote transparent access to public skills, enterprise and youth-support programmes, with clear eligibility and selection information.
  • Support digital literacy, responsible technology use and opportunities for young people to participate in online services and the wider digital economy.
  • Give attention to the needs of young entrepreneurs, artisans, traders and small businesses when relevant policies and budgets are considered.
  • Encourage practical tracking of training completion, certification, work placements and enterprise outcomes rather than counting attendance alone.

How the office will be judged

By the quality of opportunities identified and referred, the transparency of programme information, and whether public interventions report outcomes that can be checked.

04 · Pillar four

Healthcare access and social welfare

Access to basic healthcare should not depend on whether a family can afford a long and difficult journey. Primary healthcare facilities, referral systems, essential services, reliable water and appropriate staffing all matter. A legislator’s role is to help expose service gaps and press the responsible authorities for answers and action.

Proposed actions

  • Support evidence-based assessments of primary healthcare facilities, including infrastructure, staffing, equipment, water, sanitation and service availability.
  • Raise documented service gaps with the relevant health authorities and seek clear responses on priorities, timelines and responsibilities.
  • Advocate for stronger primary healthcare, maternal and child health, immunisation, health education and referral pathways within approved public programmes.
  • Encourage partnerships with qualified health institutions and organisations for outreach where such partnerships are lawful, properly coordinated and sustainable.
  • Promote attention to the needs of older people, persons with disabilities and vulnerable households in relevant social welfare discussions.
  • Monitor announced health projects by checking approval, funding, implementation and operational status before describing them as delivered.

How the office will be judged

By the facility needs documented, responsible agencies engaged, referrals and responses recorded, and the accuracy of public updates on health-related commitments.

05 · Pillar five

Education, learning and academic opportunity

Education is an investment in the capacity of the next generation. Every child deserves a safe and supportive learning environment. Schools need attention to classrooms, sanitation, learning materials, access, maintenance and the conditions that allow teachers and learners to do their work.

Proposed actions

  • Encourage a ward-informed register of school needs, developed with relevant education authorities, school communities and available evidence.
  • Advocate for fair attention to classroom rehabilitation, safe learning spaces, sanitation, furniture, learning resources and basic facilities.
  • Promote transparent information on scholarships, bursaries, examination support and other approved education assistance programmes.
  • Encourage partnerships that support mentoring, reading, science, technology, technical education and career guidance.
  • Raise concerns about barriers to attendance and learning, including accessibility and the needs of vulnerable pupils.
  • Seek public reporting that distinguishes proposed interventions from approved, funded, ongoing and completed work.

How the office will be judged

By the quality of school-needs evidence, the education issues raised through legislative channels, and transparent information on the status of programmes and projects.

06 · Pillar six

Infrastructure, water, energy and environmental resilience

Infrastructure should be judged by what it makes possible. A passable road can connect a farmer to a buyer, a pupil to school and a patient to care. Safe water supports health and productivity. Reliable energy helps small businesses operate. Drainage and maintenance protect communities and public investments.

Proposed actions

  • Work with communities to identify and rank pressing road, bridge, drainage, water and electricity needs using clear criteria.
  • Advocate for appropriate constituency attention in relevant public plans and budgets, while recognising the agencies legally responsible for delivery.
  • Encourage technical assessments and realistic project scopes before public works are announced or promoted.
  • Give maintenance and rehabilitation proper attention alongside new construction, to protect public assets and reduce waste.
  • Promote transparent project monitoring, including the responsible agency, approval and funding status, contractor where publicly available, implementation stage and completion evidence.
  • Encourage community participation in identifying environmental risks, drainage problems and practical local solutions.

How the office will be judged

By the quality of needs assessments and advocacy, the accuracy of project-status information and whether public commitments are tracked from proposal through approval and implementation.

07 · Pillar seven

Community security, peace and social cohesion

Development cannot thrive where residents feel unsafe or communities lose trust. Security is a shared public responsibility led by constituted security agencies. Effective representation should support lawful coordination, community confidence, prevention and timely attention to local concerns without encouraging vigilantism or taking over the role of security professionals.

Proposed actions

  • Maintain constructive engagement with relevant security agencies, local authorities, traditional institutions and community stakeholders within lawful boundaries.
  • Provide appropriate channels for residents to report community safety concerns to the institutions responsible for responding to them.
  • Encourage prevention-focused dialogue, youth engagement and community initiatives that strengthen trust and peaceful coexistence.
  • Raise documented security and public-safety concerns through appropriate legislative and administrative channels.
  • Promote impartiality, respect for the rule of law and protection of the rights and dignity of all residents.
  • Support accurate public communication that avoids rumour, panic, political exploitation or disclosure of sensitive operational information.

How the office will be judged

By the quality of lawful engagement, responsible referrals, constructive community dialogue and the accuracy and restraint of public communication on security matters.

From policy to public value

How the programme will be pursued

A manifesto must explain not only what it wants to achieve, but also how the work will be organised. The following sequence is a proposed operating framework, subject to the commencement of the legislative term and the procedures of the House.

First 100 days: listen, map and establish the baseline

  • Open and publicise constituency contact and casework channels.
  • Begin ward-level listening sessions across the constituency.
  • Compile an initial register of priority concerns and the public institutions responsible for them.
  • Identify available baseline information on schools, health facilities, roads, water, agriculture and youth opportunity.
  • Publish an opening report setting out issues heard, proposed next steps and matters requiring further evidence.

Year one: turn evidence into legislative work

  • Prioritise issues that can be advanced through motions, questions, petitions, committee work and budget scrutiny.
  • Engage relevant ministries, departments, agencies and community institutions.
  • Publish periodic updates showing actions taken, responses received and issues still pending.
  • Review the constituency needs register with residents and correct gaps as better evidence becomes available.

Years two and three: sustain scrutiny and partnerships

  • Track public commitments and approved projects through their actual implementation stages.
  • Continue policy advocacy and committee scrutiny on issues that require sustained attention.
  • Encourage responsible partnerships that complement, rather than replace, public accountability.
  • Report delays, constraints and unresolved matters honestly, including which institution has responsibility.

Year four: account, evaluate and report

  • Publish a constituency performance report covering legislative activity and casework.
  • Show the status of major public commitments as proposed, submitted, approved, funded, ongoing, completed or delayed.
  • Explain what worked, what did not, what remains outstanding and why.
  • Invite public feedback and make the evidence available for residents to assess.
The accountability compact

Promises must be traceable

Public confidence is strengthened when residents can distinguish an aspiration from an approved project and an announcement from a completed result. The proposed reporting standard is straightforward.

Every commitment should identify

  • The community or group that raised the need.
  • The evidence supporting the issue and the proposed response.
  • The public institution with legal responsibility.
  • The legislative, administrative or partnership action being pursued.
  • The current status, next step and any known constraint.

Every public update should distinguish

  • Proposed: an idea or need being considered.
  • Submitted: formally presented to the responsible institution.
  • Approved: authorised through the relevant process.
  • Funded: supported by an identifiable appropriation or funding source.
  • Ongoing: implementation has commenced.
  • Completed: completion is supported by evidence and, where applicable, operational handover.
A shared responsibility

Progress is built through partnership

Lasting development requires cooperation among residents, the Oyo State Government, the Oyo State House of Assembly, local government institutions, traditional authorities, community organisations, businesses, professional bodies, civil society and development partners. A constituency representative should be able to work across these relationships while remaining independent in the duty to question, scrutinise and speak up for the people.

Residents also have a vital role: identify local priorities, participate in consultations, protect public assets, demand accurate information and assess representatives by evidence. This manifesto is therefore offered as a public document for discussion and scrutiny—not a substitute for community participation or the lawful decisions of public institutions.

The commitment

I want Ibarapa East to be represented with seriousness, preparation and respect. I want our farmers’ concerns to be heard where agricultural policies and budgets are discussed; our young people to have clearer pathways to skills and opportunity; our schools and health facilities to receive evidence-based attention; our infrastructure needs to be documented and pursued; and our communities to enjoy peace, dignity and responsive public institutions.

I cannot honestly promise that one office-holder can solve every challenge or control every institution. I can commit to doing the work that the office requires: listening, learning, asking, advocating, scrutinising, following up and reporting. I can commit to treating public resources as a trust and public information as a responsibility.

Listen. Think. Advocate. Answer. That is the discipline I propose to bring to representation. The people of Ibarapa East deserve a voice that is present, a process that is transparent and a standard of service that can be examined.

Hon. Babatunde Sunday Ogunlade (BABS)
